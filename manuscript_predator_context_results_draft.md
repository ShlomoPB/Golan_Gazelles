# Predator-context Temporal Results

To examine gazelle responses to sympatric predators, cameras were classified using quartiles of site-level jackal and wolf abundance into five predator-context categories: `High jackal / Low wolf` (`6` cameras), `Low jackal / High wolf` (`6` cameras), `High both` (`3` cameras), `Low both` (`2` cameras), and `Intermediate / mixed` (`43` cameras). This created a spatially explicit predator-context framework that separates predator identity from overall predator load while retaining the background landscape of mixed sites.

Sample sizes across predator contexts were uneven. The strongest data support came from `Low jackal / High wolf` and `Intermediate / mixed` cameras, whereas `High both`, `Low both`, and especially `High jackal / Low wolf` contained relatively few cameras and few fawn detections. As a result, formal temporal comparisons for fawns were limited, and several female contrasts involving `High jackal / Low wolf` could not be tested because event counts fell below the minimum threshold.

Despite that imbalance, adult classes showed clear temporal restructuring across predator contexts. Adult males differed strongly between jackal-dominated and wolf-dominated extremes when all detections were pooled: `High jackal / Low wolf` versus `Low jackal / High wolf` male overlap was `0.694` (95% CI `0.610` to `0.778`, `p < 0.001`). This contrast was already evident in the early, hotter period (`0.619`, `0.488` to `0.750`, `p = 0.010`), indicating that male diel activity was more strongly displaced in jackal-dominated than in wolf-dominated contexts. Males also differed between `High both` and `Low both` cameras in the late, milder-cooler period (`0.751`, `0.645` to `0.856`, `p = 0.010`), suggesting that total predator pressure also shapes male timing once the season cools.

Adult females showed their clearest predator-context signal along the total predator-pressure axis rather than the jackal-versus-wolf identity axis. Female activity differed strongly between `High both` and `Low both` cameras in pooled detections (`overlap = 0.724`, `p = 0.010`), in the early hotter period (`0.608`, `p < 0.001`), and again in the later cooler period (`0.651`, `p < 0.001`). Females also differed between `Extreme predator contexts` and `Intermediate / mixed` cameras in pooled detections (`0.888`, `p < 0.001`) and in both seasonal subsets. These results suggest that female gazelles respond behaviorally not just to predator identity, but to the broader risk environment created by predator extremes.

By contrast, fawn temporal contrasts were weakly resolved. The exact jackal-dominated versus wolf-dominated comparison could not be tested because only `2` pooled fawn detections occurred in `High jackal / Low wolf` cameras. The only estimable fawn contrast was `Extreme predator contexts` versus `Intermediate / mixed`, and even there overlap remained high and non-significant in pooled detections (`0.873`, `p = 0.646`) and in the early hotter period (`0.810`, `p = 0.556`), with only a suggestive late-period difference (`0.751`, `p = 0.071`). Thus, the current data do not support strong conclusions about fawn temporal restructuring across predator contexts.

Overall, the predator-context framework reveals class-specific behavioral sensitivity to sympatric predators. Males showed the clearest separation between wolf-dominated and jackal-dominated contexts, whereas females responded more consistently to overall predator load. Fawns were too sparse in the most extreme predator contexts to yield equally strong inference. This supports the idea that predator effects on gazelles are not only class-specific in magnitude, but may also differ in whether they are driven by predator identity or by combined predator pressure.

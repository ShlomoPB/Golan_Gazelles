# Statistical Analysis

We evaluated class-specific responses of mountain gazelles to predator and anthropogenic pressures using camera-trap count data collected across 60 sites and 7 survey rounds. We analyzed three demographic classes separately: fawns, adult males, and adult females. Because detection was imperfect and varied among camera stations and rounds, all inferential models explicitly separated the ecological state process from the observation process.

Our primary analysis used class-specific negative-binomial N-mixture models implemented in `spAbundance`. We selected the non-spatial negative-binomial formulation as the main abundance framework because it provided the best balance between ecological interpretability and MCMC stability across classes, whereas spatial and multispecies abundance models were more difficult to estimate cleanly. For each class, the latent abundance process was modeled as a function of ecologically motivated site covariates describing habitat context, predator pressure, and anthropogenic pressure. Habitat covariates included `Open.Spaces`, `Nature.Reserves`, and `Built.and.Roads`; predator covariates included `null.jackal.abundance` and `Null.Wolf.Abundance`; and anthropogenic or disturbance-associated covariates included `human.abundance` and `null.boar.abundance`. All continuous site covariates were standardized prior to analysis to place coefficients on a comparable scale.

Detection probability was modeled using a common observation model across classes to avoid confounding biological differences among classes with different detection specifications. The main detection model included `Path.Type`, `Camera.Model`, `Vegetation.Structure`, `Area.Behind.Camera`, and `log_days_operational`, reflecting camera placement, immediate vegetation context, visibility or passability behind the camera, equipment differences, and sampling effort. This observation model was chosen because it was biologically defensible and outperformed simpler effort-only alternatives in exploratory model comparison.

For the class-specific abundance analysis, we compared a small candidate set of ecologically constrained models for each demographic class. Candidate models were grouped into four conceptual sets: (1) habitat-only models, (2) habitat plus predator models, (3) habitat plus anthropogenic models, and (4) habitat plus predator plus anthropogenic models. Model evaluation emphasized convergence diagnostics, posterior predictive performance, and ecological coherence of coefficient estimates, rather than reliance on a single information criterion. We interpreted posterior means and 95% credible intervals for standardized coefficients to compare responses across classes.

We used occupancy models implemented in `spOccupancy` as a secondary sensitivity analysis to test whether the direction of class-specific ecological responses was robust to modeling occurrence rather than abundance. Occupancy analyses used the same site-level ecological covariates and a detection model based on camera placement and effort variables. These models were used to support broad ecological interpretation rather than to replace the primary abundance analysis.

To evaluate whether demographic classes shared residual spatial or ecological structure after accounting for measured covariates, we additionally fit exploratory latent-factor multispecies N-mixture models using `lfMsNMix` in `spAbundance`. These models treated fawns, males, and females as separate response categories within a joint abundance framework. Because `lfMsNMix` does not allow truly different abundance formulas by class, we fit the union of class-relevant covariates and interpreted species-specific posterior coefficients. We treated these joint models as exploratory because posterior predictive fit remained weaker than for the single-class analyses and because the resulting latent correlations describe shared residual abundance structure rather than causal demographic interactions.

Our main inferential goal was therefore to determine whether demographic classes differed in their responses to key threat axes, especially predator pressure and anthropogenic landscape pressure. The strongest evidence was taken from the class-specific non-spatial negative-binomial abundance models, while occupancy models and latent-factor joint models were used as complementary sensitivity and structure analyses.

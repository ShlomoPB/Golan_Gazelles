# Temporal Activity Results

Temporal activity differed among gazelle classes, and these differences depended on season once detections were converted to apparent solar time. Across all detections pooled, fawns were temporally distinct from both adult classes, whereas adult males and females overlapped strongly. Female-fawn overlap was `0.777` (95% CI `0.731` to `0.822`, `p < 0.001`), male-fawn overlap was `0.728` (`0.679` to `0.776`, `p < 0.001`), and female-male overlap was `0.945` (`0.924` to `0.966`, `p = 0.034`; Table T1; Fig. T1). Estimated activity level from the circular kernel models was lowest for fawns (`0.364`), intermediate for females (`0.543`), and highest for males (`0.606`).

Seasonal stratification clarified these class differences. In the early, hotter part of the survey, fawns were especially distinct from adults. Female-fawn overlap fell to `0.712` (`0.638` to `0.787`, `p < 0.001`), and male-fawn overlap remained low at `0.720` (`0.650` to `0.790`, `p < 0.001`). In contrast, females and males were nearly indistinguishable in this early period (`0.956`, `0.927` to `0.985`, `p = 0.591`). In the later, milder-cooler period, the pattern shifted. Female-fawn overlap increased to `0.858` (`0.795` to `0.921`) and was no longer significantly different (`p = 0.248`), whereas female-male overlap decreased to `0.892` (`0.857` to `0.926`, `p < 0.001`). Male-fawn overlap remained comparatively low (`0.758`, `0.689` to `0.826`, `p < 0.001`). Thus, fawn-adult temporal separation was strongest in the hotter period, while male-female temporal differentiation emerged more clearly in the cooler period.

Temporal activity also shifted within classes across high- and low-pressure parts of the landscape, with the strongest restructuring again concentrated in adults (Table T2; Fig. T2). In the early, hotter period, females showed strong temporal shifts across several anthropogenic and land-use gradients, including `Orchards` (`overlap = 0.613`, `p < 0.001`), `Cropland` (`0.664`, `p < 0.001`), `Firing zones` (`0.682`, `p < 0.001`), `Human abundance` (`0.702`, `p < 0.001`), and `Built and roads` (`0.708`, `p < 0.001`). Early-period males also showed strong shifts, particularly across `Built and roads` (`0.748`, `p < 0.001`), `Jackal abundance` (`0.806`, `p = 0.007`), `Wolf abundance` (`0.837`, `p < 0.001`), and `Human abundance` (`0.839`, `p = 0.020`).

In the later, milder-cooler period, adults still restructured their activity in relation to pressure gradients, but the strongest signals were somewhat different. Females showed the clearest late-period shifts across `Built and roads` (`0.678`, `p < 0.001`), `Orchards` (`0.761`, `p < 0.001`), and `Wolf abundance` (`0.817`, `p < 0.001`). Males continued to show strong temporal shifts across `Built and roads` (`0.769`, `p < 0.001`), `Jackal abundance` (`0.774`, `p < 0.001`), `Human abundance` (`0.819`, `p < 0.001`), and `Wolf abundance` (`0.868`, `p = 0.020`). These results indicate that temporal responses to pressure are not static across the survey, but vary with seasonal context.

By contrast, fawn high-vs-low pressure comparisons remained weakly resolved because sample sizes became small after stratifying by both quartile group and season. Several juvenile comparisons could not be tested formally after applying the minimum event threshold, and the remaining comparisons generally had wider confidence intervals and non-significant kernel-test results.

Overall, the solar-time seasonal analysis supports three conclusions. First, fawns differ behaviorally from adults in diel activity. Second, the magnitude of class differences changes across the season, with stronger juvenile-adult separation in the hotter period and stronger adult sex differentiation later in the year. Third, adult classes, especially females, restructure their activity patterns across predator, human, and land-use gradients, indicating that ecological pressures are expressed not only in abundance and occupancy, but also in time use.
